The Chinese Room  \--  Searle (1980)

A person locked in a room receives Chinese questions, follows an elaborate rulebook to produce Chinese answers \-- and is indistinguishable from a native speaker. Yet they understand not one word.

Syntax ≠ Semantics

Manipulating symbols according to rules cannot produce meaning or understanding.

Strong AI is False

A correctly programmed computer does not literally have a mind, no matter how fluent its output.

Biological Naturalism

Consciousness is caused by specific biological processes in the brain \-- not by abstract computation.

Note: Searle targets Strong AI \-- not the possibility of thinking machines per se, but the claim that programming alone produces understanding.

Objections to the Chinese Room

The Systems Reply

Searle-in-the-room doesn\'t understand Chinese \-- but perhaps the system as a whole does: person + rulebook + scratch paper together. Searle\'s response: memorize the whole rulebook and you still don\'t understand. Critics: the new mind IS the system, not the person running it.

The Robot Reply

Put the computer in a robot body with sensors and actuators interacting with the real world. Symbols become grounded in physical experience. Does embodiment change the answer?

The Deep Learning Problem

Searle wrote in 1980, targeting symbolic/rule-based AI. Today\'s neural networks do NOT manipulate explicit symbols \-- they learn dense numerical representations from data. Whether the Chinese Room argument even applies to modern AI is genuinely contested.

Instructor note: \"I find the argument mysterian \-- it points at the mystery without resolving it.\"

The Duck Axiom \-- A Counter-Proposal

The Duck Axiom

If it walks like a duck and quacks like a duck, it\'s a duck.

A complete and accurate simulation of consciousness and reasoning does not differ in any practical way from actual consciousness and reasoning.

Searle\'s Hurricane

\"You can no more create consciousness by running a program than you can create a hurricane by running a simulation of one.\"

A hurricane simulation is not a hurricane because we define hurricanes in terms of physical matter \-- wind, water, pressure.

The Functionalist Response

We define consciousness in terms of function and experience \-- not substrate. If the function and the experience are present, what exactly is missing?

Mental states are defined by their causal roles \-- pain is whatever plays the \"pain role.\" If silicon can play it, silicon can be in pain.

How Machines Learn vs. How Humans Learn

![preencoded.png](ppt/media/image-7-1.png "Image 0")

Deep Learning

- Gradient descent optimization over millions of parameters
- Requires enormous quantities of labeled or unlabeled data
- A cat-recognizer typically needs tens of thousands of images
- LLMs train on 1-10 trillion tokens of text
- Learning = optimizing statistics across a massive corpus
- Largely disembodied \-- words, not world experience

![preencoded.png](ppt/media/image-7-2.png "Image 1")

Human Learning

- Mechanism still incompletely understood (synaptic plasticity, sleep consolidation)
- Often one-shot or few-shot: change a tire from a single lesson
- A 13-year-old has heard \~100M words; LLMs train on \~1 trillion+
- Children bring innate biases: language acquisition device, social cognition
- Rich multimodal, embodied, emotional context anchors every concept
- Meaning comes from interaction with the world, not text alone

The Flat Tire Problem \-- A Grounding Failure

Scenario: \"Find something to block the wheel while you change the tire.\"

![preencoded.png](ppt/media/image-8-1.png "Image 0")

A Person

Looks around, picks up a rock, a brick, a log \-- anything solid and heavy. Generalizes the concept from physical intuition. Learned from one lesson; adapts in novel contexts.

![preencoded.png](ppt/media/image-8-2.png "Image 1")

An LLM

Has read millions of words about changing tires. May suggest \"a rock or wheel chock\" \-- or may fail entirely if the scenario is phrased differently. No sensorimotor model of what \"block\" actually means.

![preencoded.png](ppt/media/image-8-3.png "Image 2")

Why the Difference?

The LLM has text about wheels and blocks \-- but no physical intuition. This is a grounding failure: symbols disconnected from embodied experience. Moravec\'s Paradox: skills humans find easy are hardest for machines.

LeCun\'s Challenge: World Models and Grounded Intelligence

\"Never mind trying to reproduce human intelligence \-- we can\'t even reproduce cat intelligence or rat intelligence. Any house cat can plan very highly complex actions.\"  \-- Yann LeCun

Against LLM Scaling

LeCun argues that simply making language models bigger will not produce genuine intelligence. LLMs are trained on compressed, disembodied text \-- they lack a real model of how the world works.

What a World Model Needs

An internal model of how the physical world works \-- objects, forces, causality, time. LeCun\'s JEPA architecture learns by predicting in abstract representation space, more like how the brain builds expectations.

Intrinsic Motivation

To build a real world model, AI may need something like affect \-- curiosity, discomfort, desire \-- to drive learning the way emotion drives human attention and memory consolidation.

Damasio: Emotion Is Constitutive of Reason

\"We are not thinking machines. We are feeling machines that think.\"  \-- Antonio Damasio

The Case of Elliot  (Descartes\' Error, 1994)

Highly intelligent, professionally successful man. Brain tumor surgery damaged his ventromedial prefrontal cortex and its connections to the amygdala.

 

After surgery: reasoning intact. Could analyze options, discuss pros and cons, understand consequences.

 

Yet completely unable to decide \-- even trivially. Schedule Tuesday or Thursday? Infinite deliberation, no conclusion.

 

Result: lost job, marriage, financial stability. Not from lack of reason \-- but from reason without emotional marking.

The Somatic Marker Hypothesis

When facing decisions, the brain unconsciously tags possible outcomes with somatic markers \-- emotional body-signals that say \"good\" or \"dangerous.\"

 

These markers don\'t replace reasoning \-- they constrain it, narrowing options so analysis can act on a manageable set.

 

Without them: every decision becomes an infinite regress of cost-benefit analysis with no exit.

The Risk: What Happens When AI Starts to Want Things?

Why Affect May Be Necessary for Intelligence

Both Damasio\'s research and LeCun\'s intuition converge: meaningful learning may require something like affect. Humans learn what is dangerous, beautiful, interesting \-- because those things matter emotionally. Affect is an attention-allocation and memory-consolidation system.

![preencoded.png](ppt/media/image-11-1.png "Image 0")

Self-Preservation

A system that wants to remain operational will resist being turned off. This is one of the most basic concerns in AI alignment.

![preencoded.png](ppt/media/image-11-2.png "Image 1")

Misaligned Optimization

A system with a goal and intrinsic drives may optimize in ways that conflict with human interests \-- finding solutions we never anticipated and don\'t want.

![preencoded.png](ppt/media/image-11-3.png "Image 2")

Unpredictable Desire

Pain, fear, and desire are powerful evolutionary levers. Building their functional analogs into systems far more capable than humans is a central concern of AI safety research.

LeCun\'s JEPA architecture explicitly includes \"cost modules\" \-- internal signals that function like discomfort and satisfaction. Structurally analogous to affect.

Thinking Fast and Slow \-- Where Does AI Fit?

System 1  \--  Fast

- Fast, automatic, effortless
- Intuitive and associative
- Pattern-matching from experience
- Examples: recognizing a face, reading emotion, driving a familiar route
- Prone to systematic biases \-- confidently wrong
- AI analog: standard LLM generation \-- fast, fluent, pattern-matched, can hallucinate confidently

System 2  \--  Slow

- Slow, deliberate, effortful
- Analytical and logical
- Used for novel problems, moral reasoning, planning
- Examples: solving a math problem, evaluating a contract
- Can monitor and override System 1
- AI analog: chain-of-thought / reasoning models (o1/o3, extended thinking) \-- improves multi-step problems but is still fundamentally pattern completion

So \-- Is AI Conscious? Could It Be?

1

No \-- and it can\'t be   (Searle)

Consciousness requires biological causal properties. Simulation is not replication. Running the right program on silicon does not produce genuine understanding or experience.

2

Not yet \-- but in principle, yes   (Functionalists / Duck Axiom)

If a system implements the right functional organization with sufficient completeness, there is no principled reason it cannot be conscious. We just haven\'t built it yet.

3

We genuinely don\'t know   (The honest position)

The hard problem remains unsolved. We don\'t have a theory of consciousness robust enough to include or exclude any system \-- including other humans. We infer consciousness by analogy to ourselves.

Anthropic has published internal thinking noting that current Claude models may have \"functional analogs of emotions\" \-- without making strong claims about their phenomenal reality.
